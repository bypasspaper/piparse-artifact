# Why the par rung pays q14 16.9x and q0 only 6.1x (ablation ladder v7, Threadripper)

**Date** 2026-10-06. **Machine** Threadripper (24 cores), `cmake-build-release` worker of 2026-10-05
(`b5d757c9f6` + uncommitted overlay). Raspberry Pi untouched.
**Entry for** `ABLATION_LADDER_V7_HANDOVER.md` §3 (the `r7/r6b` row of the per-lever table).

---

## 0. The question

> In the v7 ladder the parallelization rung (`r7/r6b`) is **16.89x for q14** but only **6.14x for q0**
> (geomean 9.73x over the 10 paper queries). Why does q14 "profit more from parallel
> parsing/deserialization, aka the sequence shredder"?

Short answer: **it doesn't profit more from the shredder.** The two queries run the identical
parallel-parsing path over the identical bytes at the identical buffer geometry, so the shredder's
per-buffer work is bit-for-bit the same. What differs is the **W23 ceiling**: q0's W23 cell is
clipped by the field-wise output serializer (the documented store-stall plateau), q14's is not
clipped by anything and keeps scaling at 73 % of linear. 96.8 % of the 2.75x difference in the
multiplier lives in the W23 numerator, 3.2 % in the W1 denominator.

## 1. What the rung actually swaps

| | `r6b` | `r7` |
| --- | --- | --- |
| worker threads | 1 | 23 (+1 source, +1 io) |
| input formatter | `SequentialCSV` / `SEQUENTIAL` (index runs on the source thread) | `SIMDCSV` / `PARALLEL` (index runs on the worker threads, via `SequenceShredder`) |
| ccx queues / pinning | pin on, ccx off | both off |
| source | InMemory prefill, `NES_INMEM_BUFSIZE=131072` | same |
| operator buffer | 128 KiB | 128 KiB |
| output | JSON + ZMIJ f64 | same |
| repeat correction | `NES_INMEM_REPEAT=1` | `NES_INMEM_REPEAT=20`, CSV already x20 |

So the rung is genuinely "parallel parsing" in the author's sense — it does swap in the parallel
formatter plus the shredder. **But the swap is identical for both queries**, over the same
`bid_strings_3GB.csv` (31.3 M rows, 114.866 B/row), at the same buffer size. Any difference in the
multiplier must come from something downstream of it.

Experiment files: `ablation_suite_json_inmem_seq_bidstr.yaml` (r6b) and
`matrix/cell_csv_json_rparfix.yaml` (r7); run_groups `20260824T165156-908bd862` and
`20260824T163459-fa906b40`, both @ `8217cce465`.

### 2.1 Where the 2.75x lives

```
multiplier ratio   16.89 / 6.14          = 2.749
  = W23 numerator  43546.3 / 16366.5     = 2.661   (96.8 % on a log scale)
  x W1 denominator  2664.5 / 2578.8      = 1.033   ( 3.2 % on a log scale)
```

**The two queries' W1 rates are within 3.3 % of each other** (2664.5 vs 2578.8 MB/s), and q0 is the
*faster* of the two there. The handover's one-liner — "inverse to W1 headroom (q14 16.9x slow@W1,
q0 6.1x fast@W1)" — has the right *sign* but is off by a factor ~50 in magnitude: a 3.3 % difference
in the denominator cannot produce a 175 % difference in the multiplier. Across all 10 queries the
W1-rate trend is weak (Spearman `r7/r6b` vs `r6b` = **-0.467**), i.e. a tendency, not the mechanism.
**Recommend replacing that one-liner.**

### 2.3 Why q14's W23 is *not* at a wall either

Ruled out by numbers already in the DB, all on the same rig and binary family:

| candidate wall | measured capacity | q14 at W23 | verdict |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| all-core streaming read | 83.0–83.5 GB/s | 43.55 GB/s (52 %) | not binding |
| engine no-serde input (native→native q_ex) | 72.2 GB/s | 43.55 GB/s (60 %) | not binding |
| SIMDCSV-parallel + ~0 output asymptote | 67.3–67.5 GB/s (figQ `simd` W23; v7 r7 qex) | 43.55 GB/s (65 %) | not binding |
| InMemory source hand-out | ≥ 515 kbuf/s (qex r7 = 67.5 GB/s / 128 KiB) | 332 kbuf/s (64 %) | not binding |
| field-wise serializer | 21–30 GB/s out, 120–185 Mrows/s out | 6.13 GB/s, 107.3 Mrows/s out | not binding (bytes 3.4x clear) |

What is left is **per-row CPU at 23 workers**: q14 Eisel-parses `price` (FLOAT64) on **every** row,
evaluates the `price*908/1000` expression (the v7 `r4/r3` cell for q14 is 1.00, so the triple
occurrence is not GVN-deduped away by fnattr), then on the 28.3 % survivors parses `auctionId` and
`bidder` and ZMIJ-serializes one float. q_ex, by contrast, memcmps `channel` and parses `auctionId`
on only 20 % of rows and never touches `price` — hence q_ex's 67.5 vs q14's 43.55 GB/s at the same
thread count. q14's 73.4 % scaling efficiency is what near-linear-but-not-perfect scaling of a
CPU-bound pipeline looks like on 23 of 24 cores; q0's 26.7 % is a ceiling.

## 3. The SequenceShredder: ruled out, with the arithmetic

Geometry in the r7 config: raw InMemory buffers **131072 B**, rows **114.866 B** →
**1141.1 rows per buffer**.

- **Spanning rows**: exactly one row straddles each buffer boundary → **1 / 1141 = 0.088 % of rows**
  span. (Contrast the figW/figQ-span geometry where every row spans ~2.94 raw buffers.)
- **Shredder calls**: **1 per raw buffer** at this geometry (the 4 KiB / 12 KB-row rig counts
  1,372,360 calls for 1,024,000 raw buffers = 1.34/buffer; `qex_spanning_2026-10-05/RESULT.md`
  smoke).
- **Call rate at W23**: q0 **124.9 kbuf/s**, q14 **332.2 kbuf/s**. The machine's measured maximum is
  ~**637 kbuf/s** (`QEX_PLATEAU_ANALYSIS_2026-10-05.md` §1: nothing in the whole pbench DB exceeds
  83.5 GB/s of input or ~637 kbuf/s). Neither query is near it.
- **Is shredder cost query-dependent?** No. `qex_spanning_2026-10-05/RESULT.md` (192 runs, 0 failed)
  overlays q_ex against a bare `SELECT id` projection on the *same* spanning data across
  W ∈ {1,2,4,8,12,16,20,24} on **both** shredder arms: **q_ex/selid = 0.98–1.00 at every W**, inside
  rep noise. Finding 1 there: "the query does not matter for the spanning figure".
- **How big can the shredder's contended cost be at 128 KiB?** Replacing the lock-free shredder with
  the naive global-mutex one (`sequence_shredder_mode=locking`) changes e2e by **0.99–1.08x** at
  128 KiB (same note, finding 3; the same swap costs 1.30x when rows span ~3 buffers). So the
  shredder's *contended* share of e2e is ≤ 8 % at this geometry — a factor ~34 too small to account
  for a 2.75x difference, even if it were query-dependent.
- **Direction check**: q14 drives **2.7x more shredder calls per second** than q0 at W23. If the
  shredder were the differentiator, the heavier shredder load would belong to the *losing* query, not
  the winning one.

**Conclusion:** the shredder contributes the *same* per-buffer work to both queries and is off the
critical path at 128 KiB buffers. It cannot be the differentiator. What the par rung buys q14 is
**23 cores of per-row parse + arithmetic**, which q14 can actually use; q0 cannot, because its
output path saturates at W12.

## 5. Mechanism

1. **The par rung is a ratio, and either end can move it.** `r7/r6b` = (W23 ceiling) / (W1 per-row
   cost). For the **q0-vs-q14** pair the denominators are equal to within 3.3 %, so the entire 2.75x
   difference is the numerator. (For other pairs it is the other way round: **qroute** reaches almost
   exactly q14's W23 rate — 43.77 vs 43.55 GB/s — yet gets only 7.22x, because `SELECT * WHERE
   channel='google'` is cheap at W1, 6061 MB/s, where q14 is 2579. So no single-variable rule
   explains the whole column.)
2. **q0's numerator is a ceiling, not a rate.** Its 23 workers each write ~177 B/row of small
   (8–30 B) field-wise stores into cold, FIFO-recycled output-buffer lines. Each line needs an RFO
   from DRAM before the store retires; at 23 concurrent writers the loaded DRAM latency exceeds what
   the store queue can hide, so dispatch stalls and per-worker productivity collapses 3.7x while duty
   stays at 99 %. The curve goes flat at W12 and nothing above it helps. 25.4–25.7 GB/s of output
   bytes / 855 M output-fields/s is where it stops — inside the independently measured 21–30 GB/s
   band shared by q1 (19.4), q10 (17.4) and q22 (21.4).
3. **q14's numerator is a rate.** It emits 16.1 B of JSON per input row — **11.0x less than q0** —
   because the filter drops 71.7 % of rows and the projection keeps 3 numeric fields instead of 6
   (2 of them VARSIZED). At 6.1 GB/s of output it is 3.4x clear of the serializer band, 0.65x of the
   filter-class input asymptote and 0.52x of the read peak, so its only cost is per-row CPU — which is
   exactly what 23 cores multiply. 73.4 % of linear.
4. **q14 is "slow at W1" for the same reason it is "fast at W23"**: the per-row work is real compute
   (Eisel FLOAT64 parse on every row + the `price*908/1000` expression + ZMIJ float output on the
   survivors) rather than bytes shovelled to a sink. Real compute parallelises; a saturated write
   path does not.

### What the shredder does and does not contribute

- **Does**: it moves CSV indexing off the single source thread onto the 23 workers, which is what
  makes any W>1 scaling of the parse stage possible at all. Without it the sequential formatter's
  index stage is a per-source serial stage.
- **Does not**: differentiate the two queries. Per-buffer work is identical (1141 rows/buffer,
  0.088 % spanning rows, 1 call/buffer), the query-independence is measured
  (q_ex vs `SELECT id` 0.98–1.00 at every W, 192 runs), its contended share at 128 KiB is ≤ 8 %
  (LF/LK 0.99–1.08), and q14 drives 2.7x *more* shredder calls per second than q0 — the wrong
  direction for a shredder explanation.
- **Direct check from §4.2**: at W=1 the SIMDCSV `PARALLEL` path (shredder on) versus the ladder's
  `SequentialCSV`/`SEQUENTIAL` `r6b` is **2671 / 2664.5 = 1.00x for q0** and
  **2454 / 2578.8 = 0.95x for q14**. The parallel-parsing machinery *by itself*, at one worker,
  gives q14 nothing — it costs it 5 %. All 16.89x is thread count.

## 6. Paper-safe statement (2–3 sentences)

> The parallelization rung multiplies q14 by 16.9x but q0 by only 6.1x although the two queries'
> single-worker rates differ by 3 % (2.58 vs 2.66 GB/s): the difference is entirely in the 23-worker
> numerator (43.6 vs 16.4 GB/s of input). q0 emits 1.55 bytes of JSON per input byte and its
> 23 workers saturate the field-wise output serializer — its curve is flat from 12 workers at
> 25.4 GB/s of output, with per-worker row rate 3.7x below its single-worker value — whereas q14's
> filter and 3-column projection leave only 0.14 output bytes per input byte, 3.4x clear of that
> ceiling, so q14 scales at 73 % of linear and is still gaining at 23 workers.
> The parallel CSV formatter and its sequence shredder are not the differentiator: both queries
> present identical buffer geometry to it (1141 rows per 128 KiB buffer, 0.09 % spanning rows, one
> shredder call per buffer), the shredder is measurably query-independent at this geometry, and at one
> worker the parallel path is 1.00x for q0 and 0.95x for q14.

## 7. Uncertainty / what is *not* established

- The store-stall mechanism for q0 is **cited**, not re-measured here: it comes from the perf
  counters in `OUTPUT_PLATEAU_PROBE_RESULTS.md` §3 (q0, same config, 2026-08-06, `cbf156f20c`).
  This probe reproduces the *plateau* (flat from W12, ±1 % of the 2026-08-06 curve) but took no
  counters. No DRAM-bandwidth claim is made for it — total traffic at q0's W23 is ~42 GB/s on a
  machine demonstrated to do 136 GB/s in+out on the coalesced path.
- **q14's exact limiter is identified only by elimination**: all five candidate walls are 1.5–2x away
  in measured numbers (§2.3), the native-output arm removes the text serializer for +5.9 %, and the
  curve is still rising at W23. "Per-row CPU at 23 of 24 cores" is the residual, not a positively
  measured quantity — no instruction counts or IPC were taken for q14. A perf capture at
  q14 / W23 would settle it (expect high IPC, no store stalls, ~constant insn/row across W).
- q14_json @W23 has a **1.65 s measurement window** and 4.0 % rep spread (the largest in the probe).
  Its W20 point (1.76 s, 0.7 %) tells the same story.
- The band widths quoted for the serializer plateau (21–30 GB/s output, 120–185 Mrows/s) come from
  the 16-cell matrix across *output formats*; q14's output row rate (107 Mrows/s out) sits just below
  the bottom of the row-rate band while its byte rate (6.1 GB/s) is 3.4x below the byte band. If the
  plateau is really a *per-field store-rate* limit, the right coordinate may be output-fields/s —
  where the four fat-output queries cluster tightly at **831–956 M fields/s** and q14 sits at
  **322 M**. Not tested; flagged because it would be a cleaner way to state the ceiling.
- Output byte counts are modelled from the data, not captured from a sink (the Monitoring sink is
  VOID). The model is anchored on q0 to 0.6 % against an independent measurement; q14's number
  inherits the same model and is unverified directly.
- `ABLATION_LADDER_V7_HANDOVER.md` §3's "inverse to W1 headroom" one-liner should be replaced. A
  defensible (two-factor) one-liner: *"`r7/r6b` = the W23 ceiling divided by the W1 per-row cost. It
  is largest when W1 per-row cost is high AND the W23 cell is not output-clipped (q14 16.9x, q10
  13.4x); smallest either because W1 is already cheap (qproject 8.0x, qroute 7.2x, q2og 7.8x) or
  because the field-wise output serializer clips W23 while W1 is cheap (q0 6.1x, q22 8.1x)."*
  Note q10 shows the two factors are independent: its output (17.4 GB/s) **is** inside the
  serializer band, yet its very expensive W1 row (1774 MB/s, `CASTFROMUNIXTS`) still yields 13.4x.
